Natalie Fratto recently posted a useful provocation: a ["Threat to Pet Matrix"](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DePQVbECcOl) for AI agents and robots. In the chart, machines are not sorted by technical capability. They are sorted by how they feel. Some land closer to pet. Some land closer to threat. Some are animal-like. Some are robot-like. The point is not taxonomy. It is perception.

That is the right way to look at the next phase of AI and robotics.

People do not meet robots as spreadsheets of capability. They meet shapes. They see eyes, limbs, gait, posture, size, speed, surface, voice, and context. They ask, often before they know they are asking: is this thing serving me, watching me, replacing me, performing for me, needing care from me, or moving through my space without asking?

The threat-or-pet reaction is not irrational. It is social cognition doing fast work. The problem is that those old human and animal shortcuts are now being applied to machines.

## The body is the first interface

Human-robot interaction research has been circling this problem for years. A recent [Frontiers framework on humanlikeness and anthropomorphism](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2026.1786256/full) argues that designers should separate "humanlikeness" as a design property from "anthropomorphism" as a psychological inference. In plain English: a company can design a robot with humanlike cues, but the human still decides what those cues mean. The machine offers signals. The person supplies mind, intent, trust, fear, or affection.

That distinction matters because AI is getting more bodies. Some bodies are literal: humanoids, quadrupeds, delivery robots, companion devices. Others are interface bodies: avatars, icons, voices, keychain devices, smart glasses, and agent cards. Meta's Muse, for example, is not a humanoid robot. But as Meta turns it into a personal agent across apps, glasses, and a small Charm device, it still gains a shape in the user's life. It becomes something you can imagine carrying, asking, delegating to, or blaming.

The Robot Age question is no longer only "what can this system do?" It is "what kind of social creature does this system appear to be?"

## Example 1: Muse and the harmless assistant problem

Meta's Muse is being presented as a personal AI agent: something that can move from answering questions toward acting on your behalf. [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/metas-muse-ai-agent/story?id=136680507) described examples such as handling product returns, navigating customer service instructions, and resolving parking tickets. [CBS News](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wiG0T7MJBMY) framed it as different from a chatbot because it can take an information task and turn it into an action, like putting reminders into your calendar.

That sounds helpful. It also changes the trust problem.

A chatbot that gives advice is one kind of risk. An agent that acts, purchases, schedules, returns, or negotiates is another. Muse does not need a scary body to become socially powerful. Its "body" is the permission surface: the app, the device, the glasses, the charm, the calendar access, the payment handoff.

This is why cute agent design can be misleading. If an AI agent is framed like a friendly sidekick, people may evaluate it emotionally as a pet while operationally giving it the authority of an assistant. The form says small. The permission set says large.

In Fratto's matrix, a system like Muse sits in the uncomfortable middle. It wants to feel like a pet, but its agency can make it feel like a threat.

## Example 2: Digit 5 and the worker-shaped bet

Agility's Digit 5 is the opposite problem. Digit does not pretend to be a pet. It is tall, bipedal, industrial, and explicitly work-oriented. It has a head-like sensor area, arms, legs, and a torso. People read it immediately as a worker because the body maps onto a human work role.

That is powerful design. It is also risky design.

Research on the uncanny valley suggests that near-human appearance and motion can trigger discomfort when the body violates expectations. One [predictive-processing account](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6556781/) argues that uncanny reactions can come from a mismatch between what a humanlike form leads us to expect and how the artificial body actually moves. Other HRI research shows that [robot posture and size](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12369-022-00878-x) influence perceptions such as dominance, competence, eeriness, and threat.

Digit's advantage is that it is legible: people understand that it walks, carries, reaches, yields, and works around human-scale infrastructure. Its risk is that the human body plan brings human social assumptions with it. A tall biped near workers is not interpreted like a cart. It is interpreted like a co-present actor.

That is why Digit 5's design challenge is not only safety certification. It is expectation management. The robot has to look competent without looking autonomous in the wrong way. It has to communicate that it has intent without implying that it has judgment.

## Example 3: Spot and the police-dog shadow

Boston Dynamics' Spot is one of the clearest cases of context changing the body.

In an industrial site, Spot can read as useful equipment: a mobile inspection platform that climbs stairs, carries sensors, and keeps people out of hazardous environments. [Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/products/spot) describes Spot as a 74.5-pound quadruped with 360-degree terrain sensing, obstacle avoidance, payload capacity, and a wide operating temperature range. In that context, the animal-like body is practical. It moves through spaces built for bodies rather than wheels.

Put a similar robot next to law enforcement and the meaning changes immediately.

The quadruped shape pulls from two directions at once. It resembles an animal, which can make it feel familiar. But it also resembles a police dog, which is not simply a pet. It is an instrument of state power. Police uses of Spot in high-risk situations are often justified as ways to keep humans out of danger. That may be true. But the same body walking into public space can trigger an entirely different social reading: surveillance, coercion, intimidation, and remote force.

This is the key translation from human threat perception into robot form. Size, speed, posture, affiliation, and implied authority matter. A robot dog at a factory is equipment. A robot dog beside police is a threat cue.

## Example 4: ElliQ and the designed non-threat

Companion robots move in the other direction. They are often designed to avoid human threat cues almost completely.

ElliQ is a good example. [Fuseproject](https://fuseproject.com/case-studies/elliq) describes it as an AI companion for older adults, designed to support daily life rather than mimic a human caregiver. It is not humanoid. It does not pretend to be a dog. It uses a small tabletop form, soft movement, voice, and screen-based interaction to suggest presence without physical dominance.

That restraint is important. In companion robotics, "pet" does not necessarily mean animal. It means low threat, low physical power, high responsiveness, and a visible request for relationship. The system feels safer because it does not have hands, legs, height, speed, or a body capable of blocking you.

This is also why some companion robots succeed emotionally despite limited capability. People are not only evaluating what the robot can do. They are evaluating whether the robot can hurt, judge, replace, embarrass, or dominate them.

The pet side of the matrix is not about cuteness alone. It is about a lack of coercive affordance.

## Example 5: Delivery robots and the public-space test

Delivery robots sit in another ambiguous zone. They are small, slow, boxy, and often intentionally cute. They do not look like people. They do not look like predators. They are designed to read as harmless public servants. As we argued in [The Delivery Robot Is Splitting in Two](/news/drone-sidewalk-delivery-public-space), the real issue is not only autonomy. It is public-space negotiation.

But a sidewalk robot still enters shared space. It rolls past children, wheelchair users, dogs, outdoor dining, cyclists, curb cuts, apartment doors, and busy corners. That means the robot can feel like a pet in product photography and like a threat in a crowded sidewalk.

The threat is not physical dominance in the humanoid sense. It is spatial entitlement. Who moves? Who yields? Who owns the curb? Who is being recorded? Who cleans up when the robot gets stuck? Who gets the convenience, and who gets the clutter?

This is where robot design becomes civic design. A delivery robot does not need a face to produce a social reaction. Its body asks for rights in public.

## Bias does not disappear when the body is made of plastic

The hardest part of the Threat to Pet Matrix is that it reveals our own bias.

Humans already make snap judgments from bodies. We read height, gait, posture, facial structure, clothing, speed, proximity, and group affiliation as cues for warmth, competence, dominance, or danger. Those judgments are often biased, racialized, gendered, classed, and culturally specific. Robots do not erase those shortcuts. They inherit them.

That means "what looks threatening" is not a neutral design question.

A tall humanoid may feel authoritative because we associate height and posture with dominance. A quadruped may feel cute in a home and coercive near police because we associate dogs with both companionship and force. A soft companion bot may be trusted because it appears weak. A delivery robot may be tolerated in one neighborhood and rejected in another because public space is already politically unequal.

HRI researchers have warned that anthropomorphism is not only about adding eyes or faces. It is about the social inferences people make from the entire system: appearance, motion, context, task, and role. The "pet" side can overproduce trust. The "threat" side can overproduce fear. Both can hide the real question: what power does the system have?

## The Robot Age view

The next design frontier is not making robots more human. It is making robots more honestly readable.

A robot should not look like a pet if it is asking for financial permission, calendar access, surveillance authority, or physical right-of-way. A robot should not look like a worker if it cannot handle the expectations that come with working beside people. A robot should not look like a harmless animal when it is attached to policing, security, or military use.

This is where Fratto's chart is useful. It gives us a language for the emotional first impression. But robot literacy has to go one step further.

The right question is not only whether a machine feels like a pet or a threat. The right question is whether the feeling matches the power.

If the machine looks cute but controls money, access, or attention, the pet cue may be deceptive. If the machine looks threatening but is only inspecting a dangerous facility, the fear cue may be contextually wrong. If the machine looks human enough to invite empathy but not competent enough to justify trust, the design is creating an expectation trap.

The shape of AI is becoming part of the interface. In the robot age, form is not decoration. It is a claim about relationship.

And people will judge that claim before the robot says a word.
