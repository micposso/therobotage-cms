Construction robots need a safety plan that everyone working around them can understand. Picture a flooring robot placing tile when another trade needs to cross the room. The installer supervising the machine knows its controls. The electrician approaching with a cart may never have seen it before. Who stops the work, and who decides when it resumes?

That hypothetical encounter is a useful lens on [FORT Robotics' October 22 funding announcement](https://www.fortrobotics.com/news/fort-robotics-secures-approximately-20-million-in-additional-funding-led-by-tiger-global-and-mark-cuban-companies). The Philadelphia company builds wireless safety controls and infrastructure for autonomous machines, with construction among its target markets. Its business addresses a question that becomes more urgent as robots enter shared workplaces: how do humans retain effective control?

The financing deserves precision. FORT announced approximately $20 million in commitments: roughly $10 million led by Tiger Global, with Mark Cuban Companies participating, expected to close that week; and another $10 million from Tiger committed to an equity financing expected to close alongside FORT's proposed business combination with Newbury Street II Acquisition Corp. Those commitments should not be described as a fully completed $20 million round.

## The installer stays in the picture

[Human Friendly Robotics](https://www.humanfriendly.bot/) offers a concrete example of how construction work is being divided. Its Tyler flooring robot handles repetitive placement with an operator alongside it. The company says the crew retains floor preparation, materials, layout decisions, cuts, edges, finishing, and quality control.

That division preserves substantial human judgment. [Floor Covering News reports](https://www.fcnews.net/2026/09/human-friendly-robotics-tackles-labor-shortage/) that an installer establishes the first row's alignment and squareness; Tyler then uses installed material as a reference. The robot depends on a workflow that skilled installers establish and support.

For a product manager, that makes the crew the unit of analysis. How much time goes into staging material, supervising placement, and resolving interruptions? Does the operator have capacity for those responsibilities, or has tending the robot been added to an already full workload? Installation speed alone cannot answer either question.

## A stop button needs a shared meaning

FORT's [Wireless E-Stop Pro](https://www.fortrobotics.com/news/introducing-the-wireless-e-stop-pro-next-generation-worksite-safety) gives a worker a wearable way to send a stop command to a connected machine. FORT says the product has third-party SIL 3 certification under IEC 61508, a functional-safety standard. That is a component-level claim, not certification of an entire construction operation.

For UX designers, the important distinction is between requesting a stop and knowing the machine has reached the required safe state. A display that acknowledges a button press must not leave an installer guessing whether movement has actually ceased. Nor should silence or an inactive screen invite someone to assume a machine is safe to approach.

Restart deserves equal attention. In the crossing scenario, releasing a control cannot stand in for checking that the work area is clear. The robot supplier, integrator, and site safety lead need to define the restart conditions and authority. The interface then needs to make those conditions legible to the operator and nearby trades.

Emergency stops also have limits: they do not replace guarding or the energy-isolation procedures required for servicing. OSHA's [industrial robot safety guidance](https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-4-safety-hazards/chapter-4) emphasizes application-specific risk assessment throughout a system's life. Construction deployments need their own assessment against the site's hazards and applicable requirements.

## Put interruptions into the pilot brief

The Robot Age's recommendation is to evaluate the work around the robot as deliberately as the task it automates. Before a construction pilot, product managers, UX designers, installers, and the site safety lead should resolve four questions together:

- **Who shares the work area?** Include material deliveries, other trades, and supervisors. Identify access routes and who updates the plan when the site changes.
- **Who can stop work and authorize restart?** Document the roles, controls, and verification steps. Do not make familiarity with a tablet interface the only route to raising a safety concern.
- **What happens when communication fails?** Have qualified personnel validate the system's specified response to lost connections and show operators how that state is communicated.
- **What does recovery cost the crew?** Record assistance time, interruptions, and rework alongside output. Test whether status information remains understandable with gloves, noise, dust, and the languages used on site.

These are proposed acceptance criteria, not reported results from either company. A useful pilot would reveal whether installers can identify why work stopped, whom to contact, and what must happen next without improvising around the machine.

Construction robots earn their place on a crew when the people around them have clear authority over the next movement.
