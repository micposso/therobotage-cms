Agility Robotics has the thing most humanoid companies still want: proof that a bipedal robot has done real work in real industrial facilities.

That does not mean the business case is proven. It means the question has changed. Digit is no longer only a robot-design story. It is now an economics story.

Agility's latest SPAC materials with Churchill Capital Corp XI describe a company trying to convert real deployments, a new generation of hardware, and more than $300 million in multi-year Digit 5 orders into a scaled humanoid business. The pitch is direct: Digit 4 proved that a humanoid can work in logistics and manufacturing environments; [Digit 5](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/content/agility-unveils-digit-5-humanoid-robot-built-for-cooperatively-safe-work-at-scale) is supposed to prove that a humanoid can scale.

That is the right test. The humanoid race is no longer about whether a robot can walk on video. It is about whether the robot can earn its keep.

## The business is still early

Agility is going public around a large future narrative. The proposed deal values the company at a $2.5 billion pre-money equity value and is expected to provide more than $620 million in gross proceeds, according to the [June 2026 Churchill investor presentation filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2074973/000121390026071287/ea029548401ex99-2.htm).

The same materials emphasize real traction: nine committed customer facility deployments, 65,000 hours of operations, a RoboFab facility designed for 10,000 robots per year, and more than $300 million in committed Digit 5 orders. But those orders are not current revenue. The footnote matters: Agility says the order figure reflects expected multi-year value, subject to contractual milestones, and relates to a 1,000-robot, three-year RaaS contract.

[The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-robotics-reports-18m-revenue-ahead-of-humanoid-spac) later highlighted the gap between the ambition and the current base: Agility generated $1.8 million in 2025 net sales against a $140 million operating loss, with roughly $100 million of cash burn. That is not unusual for a company trying to industrialize a new robotics category. But it is the whole story: Agility has evidence. It does not yet have scale.

The SPAC is therefore not a celebration lap. It is fuel for the harder phase: manufacturing, deployment density, service cost, customer retention, and repeatable ROI.

## Digit 5 changes the economic argument

Digit 5 is the robot Agility wants investors and customers to underwrite.

The upgrade is not cosmetic. Agility says Digit 5 is built for "cooperatively safe" work near people, using human detection, safety cues, and independent safe motion control to reduce reliance on traditional guarding. It also claims a 50 lb payload, a 90-minute battery that charges in nine minutes, a 10:1 run-to-charge ratio, swappable end-effectors, a 5'11" human-scale body, and a 7.2-foot reach.

Those specs matter because they broaden the economic surface area. Digit 4 has mostly been associated with tote handling. Digit 5 is being positioned for decanting, palletizing and depalletizing, machine tending, kitting, sequencing, inspection, and broader material flow.

That is the bet: the robot becomes more valuable when it is not a single-purpose machine. If one humanoid body can learn more workflows over time, the customer does not have to buy a new fixed automation cell for each task. Agility keeps the same basic robot, adds skills through software, and captures more lifetime value through Agility Arc, deployment services, maintenance, and fleet expansion.

In the investor deck, Agility frames two adoption models:

- **Robots-as-a-Service:** roughly $8,500 per month, or about $100,000 per year, plus a deployment fee, producing about $500,000 in illustrative revenue to Agility over a five-year useful life.
- **Ownership plus software and maintenance:** upfront robot purchase, deployment fee, and recurring services, producing about $400,000 in illustrative revenue over five years.

Those numbers are not guarantees. The filing says they are illustrative, built on management assumptions. But they show what Agility needs Digit 5 to become: not a one-time hardware sale, but an industrial asset with recurring software, support, and workflow value.

## The customer math is simple and fragile

Agility's customer case is built against labor. The SEC presentation compares Digit 5 to a fully burdened human labor cost of about $30.50 per hour, across two 10-hour shifts per day, 120 hours per week. Under that model, the company argues that a $100,000 annual RaaS cost can replace about $200,000 of annual labor value and create roughly $100,000 of annual customer savings.

That is a clean slide. Real facilities are messier.

The savings only appear if Digit is assigned to work that actually needs those hours, if it stays productive, if it does not introduce too much supervision or recovery overhead, if service costs stay controlled, and if the customer can reorganize workflow around the robot. A humanoid that works well for a tightly scoped tote path is valuable. A humanoid that must be babysat, fenced off, frequently reset, or limited to one narrow task becomes expensive very quickly.

This is why the deployment evidence matters more than the headline valuation.

Agility says Digit has more than 65,000 hours of real-world operation and work across customer sites including GXO, Schaeffler, Amazon, and Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada. At GXO's Flowery Branch facility near Atlanta, Agility says Digit 4 reached a 100,000-tote milestone with about 98% on-task accuracy. Agility also describes [Digit's GXO deployment](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/content/digit-deployed-at-gxo-in-historic-humanoid-raas-agreement) as a long-term commercial role in a facility near Atlanta.

That is the most important part of the Agility story. Not the humanoid shape. Not the SPAC. The hours.

## The real U.S. deployment story is still concentrated

The most concrete U.S. deployment is GXO. In 2024, GXO and Agility announced a multi-year agreement to deploy Digit in GXO logistics operations after a proof-of-concept pilot, with the first live work tied to a Spanx facility in Georgia. [GXO's announcement](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/gxo-signs-industry-first-multi-203000303.html) described Digit robots and Agility Arc working under a RaaS model, coordinating tasks such as moving totes from cobots to conveyors.

That is real deployment, but it is not yet a broad labor replacement story. It is a narrow industrial workflow where the value proposition is easier to test: repetitive material movement, known facility layout, controlled handoffs, measurable throughput, and existing automation islands that need connection.

Amazon is the more complicated signal. Agility lists Amazon among its customers and strategic investors, and Digit has been tested in Amazon-linked contexts. But Amazon is also one of the world's most sophisticated builders and operators of warehouse automation. If Amazon is learning from Digit, that is validation. It is also a reminder that the buyer may eventually want internal leverage, not dependency.

Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada and Schaeffler add industrial credibility. They suggest the use case is not only e-commerce totes. But the publicly visible pattern is still early: pilots, facility-specific deployments, and a narrow set of repetitive work.

That should not be held against Agility. This is how serious robots enter the world. The danger is treating the first commercial deployments as if they already prove mass adoption.

## Why bipedal is the strategy, not the stunt

The easy critique is that wheels are cheaper. Most of the time, they are. Warehouses already use AMRs, conveyors, arms, shuttles, lifts, and fixed automation. A bipedal robot carries all the complexity of balance, gait, foot placement, actuation, fall risk, safety validation, and whole-body control.

So why bet the company on legs?

Because Agility is not trying to build the cheapest way to move one object along one path. It is trying to build a robot body that can enter environments designed for people without the facility being rebuilt around the robot. That is the strategic case for a humanoid biped:

- **Human-scale access:** Digit can work around shelves, totes, conveyors, aisles, carts, pallets, doorways, and workstations built for people.
- **Vertical reach:** A human-height body can interact with the built environment without every handoff being redesigned.
- **Workflow flexibility:** A walking, lifting, reaching machine can theoretically move between tasks as software skills improve.
- **Deployment density:** If one platform can cover more jobs at a site over time, the customer can expand within a facility instead of buying a separate automation island for each workflow.
- **Labor substitutability:** The humanoid form maps directly to jobs already written around human motion, even if the robot is not humanlike in intelligence.

That is Agility's central insight: the humanoid is not just a marketing shape. It is a compatibility layer for a world already built at human scale.

The risk is that compatibility is expensive. If a wheeled robot can do the same job, the biped loses. If fixed automation can do the same job more reliably, the biped loses. If the humanoid's extra mobility does not create enough additional workflow coverage, the economics compress.

Agility's strategy only works if legs unlock enough useful work to justify their complexity.

## The manufacturing challenge is the business challenge

Agility's RoboFab facility in Salem, Oregon is designed for up to 10,000 robots per year at full capacity. The investor deck also says roughly 75% of Digit parts are sourced from the U.S. That matters in a world where robotics is increasingly tied to supply-chain resilience, defense-adjacent policy, and industrial sovereignty.

But a factory designed for 10,000 units is not the same thing as a factory producing 10,000 useful, serviceable, margin-positive robots.

The SEC materials make the manufacturing risk explicit. Agility says it has limited experience commercializing Digit at scale and no experience in high-volume manufacturing. It also warns that Digit 5 remains in development and that customer orders are subject to milestones. Those are not throwaway legal lines. They are the main risks.

Humanoid robots are mechanically intense products. Cost down is not automatic. Batteries, actuators, sensors, compute, safety systems, service parts, field support, reliability testing, and customer-specific integration all fight the margin story. Agility's deck shows a path to lower bill-of-material costs as volume rises, but the word "path" is doing a lot of work.

Digit 5 will be judged by boring metrics: uptime, failures per shift, service response, parts cost, deployment time, human supervision burden, customer renewal, and expansion per site.

## The Robot Age view

Agility's strategy is coherent. That is the strongest thing to say about it.

The company is not selling a general-purpose household fantasy first. It is selling a bipedal industrial body into places where human-scale movement still matters: warehouses, logistics sites, factories, and material-flow environments with repetitive work and labor pressure. It has real deployments. It has a factory. It has a cloud fleet layer. It has a new robot designed around safer work near people. It has customers willing to sign orders if milestones are met.

But the economics are still a bet. The current revenue base is tiny compared with the valuation and the deployment targets. The order book is not revenue. The RaaS model depends on utilization. The ownership model depends on customers believing a humanoid can retain value over a five-year life. The whole strategy depends on Digit 5 turning bipedal complexity into facility-level flexibility.

That makes Agility one of the most interesting humanoid companies precisely because it is past the demo phase and not yet past the proof phase.

Digit 5 is not a victory lap. It is the first serious test of whether legs can be more than a spectacle.

If Agility is right, the humanoid robot becomes an adaptable labor platform for spaces already built around humans. If Agility is wrong, Digit becomes an expensive reminder that looking compatible with human work is not the same as being economically deployable.

That is the real SPAC question. Not whether humanoids are coming. Whether one of them can finally pay for its legs.
