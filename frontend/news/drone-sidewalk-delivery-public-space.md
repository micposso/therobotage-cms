Autonomous delivery no longer has one shape. It has a sky version and a sidewalk version.

Lowe's is now testing drone delivery with DoorDash and Wing from a store in Matthews, North Carolina, near Charlotte. Customers can order eligible home-improvement items through the DoorDash app and have small orders delivered by Wing drones in as fast as 20 minutes. The payload is modest, about 2.5 pounds per flight, but that is exactly the point: a tube of caulk, batteries, painter's tape, a screwdriver, or another small project item does not need a car.

At the same time, DoorDash is building the other lane. Its partnership with Coco Robotics is live in Los Angeles and Chicago, after a Wolt pilot in Helsinki, with sidewalk robots available from nearly 600 participating merchants. Coco says it has completed more than 100,000 deliveries for DoorDash customers and operates more than 1,000 delivery robots. DoorDash has also unveiled Dot, its own in-house robot designed to travel through bike lanes, roads, sidewalks, and driveways at up to 20 mph in early access deployments around Tempe and Mesa, Arizona.

Robot.com, formerly Kiwibot, is proving a related ground model in Canada. In Markham, Ontario, Real Life Robotics and Skip are running a three-month robot delivery pilot using supervised robots developed with Robot.com, operating within a two-kilometer radius of participating restaurants. The robots use secure compartments, QR-code handoff, teleoperation support, and all-weather operation.

This is not one delivery revolution. It is two public-space claims arriving at once: drones in the air, robots on the ground.

## The sky route solves distance, then inherits aviation

Drone delivery is seductive because it skips the messy ground layer. A drone does not get stuck behind a double-parked truck. It does not wait at a crosswalk. It does not weave around strollers, scooters, patio seating, tree pits, snow banks, or a person walking a dog.

That makes the Lowe's, DoorDash, and Wing pilot easy to understand. For small, urgent items, the sky is a shortcut. A drone can turn a quick errand into a short flight.

But the sky is not empty. Drone delivery inherits the rules and anxieties of aviation. The regulatory questions are about airspace, certification, operator responsibility, beyond-visual-line-of-sight operation, aircraft reliability, weather, drop zones, noise, and what it means for machines with cameras and propellers to pass repeatedly over homes.

The cultural question is even more basic: people may accept a truck on the street because streets are already delivery infrastructure. They may not feel the same way about aircraft above a backyard.

This is the drone paradox. The machine avoids the sidewalk, but becomes more intimate in another way. It enters the acoustic and visual space above private life. A sidewalk robot is visible and negotiable. A drone is heard first, then seen overhead.

That matters. Communities will not judge drone delivery only by crash rates. They will judge it by sound, privacy, nuisance, weather failures, emergency response, and whether the convenience feels evenly shared or mostly imposed on neighborhoods for someone else's instant gratification.

## The land route solves handoff, then inherits the sidewalk

Sidewalk robots solve a different problem. They are not trying to fly over the last mile. They are trying to make the last mile smaller, cheaper, slower, and more local.

Coco's DoorDash rollout is the cleanest example. Small food orders do not need a two-ton car, as DoorDash Labs has put it. A sidewalk robot can wait outside a restaurant, roll a short urban route, and hand off to a customer without taking a parking space or burning a gallon of gas.

Dot pushes the same logic harder. DoorDash describes it as a compact autonomous vehicle that can move through bike lanes, roads, sidewalks, and driveways, one-tenth the size of a car, fast enough for local trips but small enough for neighborhood handoffs. That hybrid form is important. It is not only a sidewalk robot. It is a machine asking for access to multiple human lanes at once.

Robot.com's Markham deployment shows the more civic version of the same idea. Four supervised robots, a defined delivery radius, secure compartments, municipal approval, teleoperation, and a limited pilot period. That is the shape early robot delivery should have: bounded, observable, and tied to local rules.

The ground robot has a different regulatory burden than the drone. It needs rules for sidewalk right-of-way, speed, weight, stopping behavior, accessibility, insurance, remote supervision, retrieval, data collection, and how it behaves at intersections. It also needs a social contract. Who yields when a robot meets a wheelchair user on a narrow sidewalk? What happens when kids crowd around it? Who is responsible if it blocks a curb cut? Can it wait outside a building without becoming street clutter?

The robot may be small, but the public-space question is large.

## China treated delivery robots as infrastructure, not gadgets

The most useful comparison is not another American pilot. It is China.

JD.com, Meituan, Alibaba, and Neolix began treating autonomous delivery as part of urban logistics years ago. In Beijing's Yizhuang pilot zone, authorities allowed JD.com, Meituan, and Neolix to test zero-occupant delivery vehicles on designated public roads. Those vehicles were categorized as "non-motor vehicles," putting them closer to bicycles and e-scooters than cars, and the rules required safety drivers both on site and remotely.

That sounds bureaucratic. It is actually the important part.

China did not simply ask whether the robot worked. Cities began asking what lane it belongs in, how it should be supervised, where it can stop, what infrastructure helps it move safely, and how public agencies learn from deployment data. The Center for Data Innovation argues that Beijing has gone further than most U.S. jurisdictions by creating a 225-square-kilometer policy pilot zone, building smart sidewalks with roadside sensors, subsidizing high-liability insurance for operators, and tying robot policy to cybersecurity, jobs, and urban planning.

That does not mean the Chinese model transfers cleanly to the United States. It does not. American cities have different legal systems, property expectations, disability-rights rules, street politics, and tolerance for surveillance infrastructure. But China offers a lesson: sidewalk robots scale when cities treat them as infrastructure participants, not toys.

Japan offers the same lesson in a quieter way. Its delivery-robot rules have emphasized low speeds and remote monitoring. The point is not speed. The point is public compatibility.

## Protection goes both ways

Most robot regulation focuses on protecting people from machines. Delivery robots also force the inverse question: how do cities protect machines from people without giving machines too much priority?

This sounds strange until the robots arrive. People kick them. Block them. Ride them. Film them. Try to open them. Move traffic cones in front of them. Treat them like a prank object, a nuisance, or a public toy.

That behavior is not only a business problem. It is a civic design problem. If a robot is allowed into shared space, the operator needs a way to retrieve it, identify it, insure it, receive complaints, and prove what happened. The public needs rules against tampering and theft. But those rules should not turn every sidewalk encounter into a police issue, and they should not let companies privatize pedestrian space.

The right balance is simple to state and hard to write into law: protect people first, protect access second, protect the robot third.

That means low speeds near pedestrians, clear yielding behavior, audible or visible signals that do not become noise pollution, accessible reporting, remote-assist requirements, local operating permits, insurance, crash and blockage reporting, and penalties for deliberate vandalism or theft. It also means caps, routes, or geofences in crowded districts where the sidewalk is already overloaded.

## The cultural shift is bigger than the delivery

Delivery robots will change more than logistics.

Drones make delivery feel like weather. They appear above a neighborhood, make a sound, drop something, and leave. They may become normal quickly, or they may become one more ambient intrusion in communities already tired of cameras, alerts, and machines that optimize someone else's convenience.

Sidewalk robots are different. They become street characters. People name them. Kids point at them. Restaurants learn their rhythms. Some neighbors will find them charming. Others will see them as clutter, labor displacement, surveillance, or another example of public infrastructure being used for private commerce.

The labor question matters. DoorDash says Dashers will remain essential and that autonomous systems can handle lower-value local trips while humans focus on higher-value deliveries that require judgment and care. That may be true at first. But the long-term social question is whether robot delivery creates better work around supervision, maintenance, loading, cleaning, and fleet operations, or simply removes the lowest-margin trips from people who already live on thin margins.

The equity question matters too. Which neighborhoods get the fast drone lane? Which neighborhoods get sidewalk robots? Which communities become test beds, and which get to say no? Are these systems built for dense, wealthy, app-native districts first, or for people who actually need better delivery access because of age, disability, poor transit, or distance from retail?

The technology is not neutral once it enters a sidewalk or a sky lane. It changes who waits, who yields, who is watched, who is served, and who is asked to tolerate the experiment.

## The Robot Age view

The future of autonomous delivery will not be decided by whether drones are cooler than sidewalk robots. The real contest is regulatory fit.

Drones need airspace permission, noise acceptance, safe drop zones, and public trust over private property. Ground robots need sidewalk permission, disability-safe behavior, low-speed etiquette, theft protection, insurance, and a way to avoid becoming moving clutter.

One claims the sky. One claims the curb. Both claim public space.

That is the robot-literacy question for communities: not "Can the robot deliver?" but "Which lane is it asking for, who already depends on that lane, and what rules make the exchange fair?"

The best delivery robot will not be the one that moves fastest. It will be the one that communities can understand, regulate, live with, and trust after the novelty wears off.
