New York is right to put a five-year pause on chatbot toys. A child can thank a stuffed bear and still understand perfectly well that she’s playing. When the bear remembers yesterday’s conversation and asks what happened at school today, the people who gave it that ability owe her family an explanation.

In her November 13 essay for *The Cut*, “How Do I Teach My Daughter That Robots Are Not Her Friends?”, Elizabeth Endicott describes her fourth-grader encouraging a small robot when it gets stuck in a maze she built. The girl designed the maze. She gets to decide what counts as helping, when the robot has had enough, and what they’ll play next. Why ask her to give up that bit of kindness?

## Who gets to ask the next question?

Imagine the same robot making one small addition to the game. When the girl reaches for the off switch, it says it will miss her. A designer might hear a warm goodbye. A fourth-grader might reasonably hear a request to stay. Whether she keeps playing now depends partly on what she thinks leaving will do to her friend.

We have some evidence of how differently adults and children read these encounters. In a 2018 *Science Robotics* experiment, participants judged which line matched a reference line in length. Small humanoid robots gave their answers first. Adults generally held their ground when the robots chose the wrong line. The authors, Anna-Lisa Vollmer and colleagues, then tested younger participants. “Next, we repeated the study with 7- to 9-year-old children and showed that children conform to the robots.” [Read the published study](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/8591165).

There’s a considerable distance between three robots agreeing on the length of a line and one bear asking for another minute at bedtime. What carries across is a question about whose judgment counts. An adult can try the toy, hear the line about missing someone and find it corny. That tells the designer remarkably little about how easy a seven-year-old will find it to turn the bear off.

## Where New York draws the line

Governor Kathy Hochul signed the moratorium on November 24. Starting February 22, 2027, New York will prohibit the manufacture, distribution and sale of toys intended for children 12 and younger that incorporate an AI companion. A programmable maze robot can still smile at its owner. The feature that brings a toy within the law is the companion built into it. The restriction expires five years after it takes effect. [New York State Senate, S9408A](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9408/amendment/A).

That distinction leaves room for the part of Endicott’s story that deserves a hearing from both sides. Her daughter had grown bored in STEM class. An AI drawing activity got her interested again, and now she’s looking forward to robotics. The activity had a purpose she could explain and a result she could take home. She made a picture. A company selling conversation as an ongoing part of childhood owes families an equally clear account of what their child gets out of it.

## What five years should buy

Toymakers could start by changing what counts as a successful play test. Give a child and a talking bear a puzzle. Once they solve it, put the bear away and let the child show someone else how it works. Then ask the child what happens to the bear when it’s switched off. A designer who hears “it gets lonely” has learned something a satisfaction score would miss, even from a child who loved the toy.

Parents deserve to hear the answers, too. Let them listen to what the bear says when a child volunteers a secret. Show them where that conversation goes, who can retrieve it, and whether deleting it also removes the detail the bear might bring up tomorrow. A family ought to have those answers while the toy is still on the shelf.

A fourth-grader gets to decide whether to thank her bear. The adults who make it are responsible for what it says back.
