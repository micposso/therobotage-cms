A parent shopping for a talking bear can squeeze its paw and listen to a sample answer. It is much harder to judge what the bear will say a month later, when a child who has come to trust it asks for advice. New York is right to give families a five-year pause on the sale of toys with built-in AI companions. Manufacturers are in a better position than parents to test how those conversations unfold, and they should be prepared to share the results.

Governor Kathy Hochul signed the measure on November 24. Beginning February 22, 2027, it will bar the manufacture, distribution and sale in New York of children’s toys that incorporate an AI companion. The law covers toys intended for children 12 and younger. A robot that follows a route a child programs can still be sold. What brings a toy within the law is the companion embedded in it. [New York State Senate, S9408A](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9408/amendment/A).

## The difference a reply makes

In her November 13 essay for *The Cut*, “How Do I Teach My Daughter That Robots Are Not Her Friends?”, Elizabeth Endicott describes her fourth-grader building mazes for a small robot and encouraging it when it gets stuck. The girl decides what the robot is trying to do and what kind of encouragement it needs. Her generosity is part of the pleasure she takes in a game of her own invention.

Give that robot the ability to say it missed her yesterday, and someone else now has a hand in the game. A writer or designer may intend the greeting as a courtesy. A child could hear it as a reason to come back tomorrow. Both readings are plausible. Only one of the people involved knows how the greeting was produced.

A 2018 experiment by Anna-Lisa Vollmer and colleagues offers a reason to take that difference seriously. Participants had to identify which of several lines matched a reference line in length. A group of small humanoid robots answered first and sometimes chose the wrong line. Adults generally resisted the group’s influence. The researchers wrote, “Next, we repeated the study with 7- to 9-year-old children and showed that children conform to the robots.” [Vollmer and colleagues, *Science Robotics*](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/8591165).

The experiment measured a judgment children made in front of a group. Whether a child develops an attachment to a single toy over months of play is a separate question. What the study gives designers is a reason to test with children directly. Even on a question with a visibly correct answer, the adult participants were a poor guide to how younger users would respond.

## What manufacturers should demonstrate

A five-year restriction has a cost. A well-designed conversational toy might help a child practice a second language between lessons or talk through a puzzle when an adult is busy. Endicott’s daughter regained her enthusiasm for STEM through an AI drawing exercise. There is a credible case for giving children access to such experiences. The larger claim, that a toy serves children better when it also presents itself as their companion, deserves evidence of its own.

For a company selling a language-learning toy, that evidence could begin with a simple comparison. Give two groups of children the same practice with the same voice and vocabulary. Enable the companion features for one group. A week later, see which words the children can use with another person. Ask them, too, what they think happens to the toy when they stop playing. Longer sessions are an ambiguous result if some children stayed because they thought leaving would hurt the toy’s feelings.

Families also need an account of what happens when a conversation becomes personal. Suppose a child tells a toy about an argument at school and the toy brings it up the following week. Its maker should be able to explain what it retained, who had access and how a parent can erase the detail. Those answers belong in the information a family can read before buying the toy.

When lawmakers revisit the ban, manufacturers should be ready to show what children gained from the companionship they were sold, what children understood about it, and how readily they could walk away. That is a fair burden to place on a company asking a child to trust its voice.
