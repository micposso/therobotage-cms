An alleged incident in Germany raises a question that will become increasingly urgent as robots enter public life: when a machine harms someone, who must answer for its actions?

*Header image: AI-generated editorial illustration of a remotely operated robot in an urban public space. This image does not depict the Cologne incident.*

A robot can put distance between a person and their actions. It should not put responsibility beyond reach.

According to Cologne police, a robot allegedly approached a 16-year-old girl from behind during the night of October 11, touched her inappropriately, and made sexually suggestive movements. Its 16-year-old owner and a 19-year-old companion allegedly filmed, livestreamed, and commented on the encounter. Officers seized the robot and two mobile phones. Investigators are examining how the machine operated and whether someone deliberately controlled its movements. [Cologne police statement](https://embed.presseportal.de/de/12415/article/6369503)

The distinction matters. The available police account does not establish that the robot independently selected this behavior. Treating the incident as evidence of an autonomous machine deciding to harass someone would go beyond what is known.

Yet the case raises a question that extends well beyond one investigation.

As robots enter homes, workplaces, hospitals, restaurants, and public spaces, how do we ensure that responsibility remains clear when something goes wrong?

## A human appearance does not mean independent agency

A humanoid robot can walk, gesture, and interact with people while a human controls some or all of its behavior. Its appearance tells a bystander very little about who is making the decisions.

For accountability, that distinction is fundamental.

Legal experts interviewed by *beck-aktuell* described the robot in the Cologne case as an instrument through which a person could act, rather than a criminal actor itself. They explained that existing criminal offenses may apply to the people involved, with the precise assessment depending on the evidence and circumstances. [Beck Aktuell](https://www.beck-aktuell.de/heute-im-recht/rechtsgeschehen/koeln-roboter-taeter-werkzeug-sexuelle-belaestigung-2026-10-14)

Executing a programmed movement is also different from independently choosing an objective. An investigation needs to establish who selected the behavior, who activated it, and what control they exercised.

Describing an event simply as “a robot did it” can obscure the decisions that made it possible.

## When autonomy complicates the picture

Consider three hypothetical situations: a delivery robot strikes a pedestrian after failing to detect an obstacle; a hotel robot misinterprets an interaction and makes unwanted physical contact; a household robot loses its balance while carrying an object and injures someone.

In each case, the machine might be operating without a person directing every movement. But that alone does not explain why the harm occurred.

Was there a hardware defect? A software failure? An unsafe modification? Was the robot operating in an environment it was never designed to handle? Did someone ignore a known limitation?

These questions point toward different parts of the system: the manufacturer, software provider, integrator, deploying organization, or operator. Several decisions may have contributed to the same outcome.

The task is to reconstruct that chain of decisions and determine which obligations applied. It is not enough to identify the last person who touched a controller—or to assume that whoever owns the machine must bear all responsibility.

> Autonomy does not eliminate the need for accountability. It makes traceability more important.

## Legal liability and ethical responsibility ask different questions

Criminal responsibility concerns offenses and the requirements for establishing them. Civil liability concerns legal responsibility for harm and remedies such as compensation. Ethical responsibility asks what people and organizations should have done to prevent foreseeable harm, including failures that may never result in a successful legal claim.

The European Union’s evolving framework addresses parts of this challenge.

Article 26 of the AI Act sets out duties for deployers of qualifying high-risk AI systems, including competent human oversight and operational monitoring. Its relevance to a particular robot depends on the system’s classification and the applicable implementation timetable. [European Commission’s AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26)

Separately, the revised Product Liability Directive explicitly covers software, including AI systems. Member states must transpose it into national law by December 9, 2026; the new rules apply to products placed on the market or put into service from that date. Earlier products remain subject to the previous framework. [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/defective-products-liability.html)

These measures address different issues. Neither should be presented as a universal answer to every incident involving a robot.

For organizations deploying robots, the practical challenge starts before a legal dispute: deciding what behavior is acceptable, anticipating misuse, and making intervention possible.

## Safety must include dignity and personal boundaries

Collision avoidance, force limits, mechanical reliability, and emergency stops are essential. They do not exhaust what people need from a machine sharing their space.

A robot can intimidate someone, invade their personal space, or become a vehicle for humiliation without causing a conventional mechanical injury. Recording and broadcasting an encounter can add another dimension to the harm.

Responsible deployment therefore needs to consider both accidental behavior and deliberate misuse.

A person encountering a robot on a public sidewalk may not know whether it is remotely controlled, recording them, or capable of initiating physical contact. They may have no obvious way to decline an interaction or find the person responsible.

Organizations should account for that uncertainty. People should not have to understand a robot’s technical architecture to express a boundary or ask for help.

## Make accountability visible

At The Robot Age, we believe deployment readiness should include clear answers to a few practical questions:

- Who is responsible for the robot’s operation?
- What interactions and physical contact are permitted?
- Who can intervene, and how quickly?
- What happens when the robot behaves unexpectedly?
- How can someone report an incident and receive a response?
- What records will help establish what happened?

Those answers should shape operating procedures, staff training, and interaction design before the robot enters a shared environment.

We also propose a **Robot Accountability Passport**: a visible identifier that connects a public-facing robot to its responsible organization, operational purpose, and incident-reporting channel.

Where practical, a separate status indicator could explain whether the robot is operating autonomously, receiving remote assistance, or under direct human control. Because control can change during an interaction, that information would need to reflect the current operating mode.

This is a proposal for public transparency. It would complement regulation, insurance, and internal safety processes.

The public information should be simple. The underlying records should be sufficient to investigate an incident: relevant commands, changes in control, software versions, and interventions, with appropriate privacy protections and retention limits.

Accountability should not depend on someone finding a social media video after the fact.

## The next test for robotics is public trust

Progress in locomotion, manipulation, perception, and embodied AI expands what robots can do. Successful adoption also depends on how people experience those capabilities.

A robot that performs its task while making people feel unsafe, powerless, or unable to refuse an interaction has exposed a deployment problem.

The Cologne investigation may ultimately establish deliberate human misconduct carried out through a machine. Its outcome should be determined by evidence.

The broader lesson is already worth confronting: physical distance, technical complexity, and automated behavior must not become excuses for avoiding responsibility.

The organizations putting robots into human environments should be prepared to explain how those systems operate, intervene when necessary, investigate failures, and respond meaningfully to the people affected.

**The real question is whether the people and organizations putting robots into our world are prepared to accept responsibility for what those machines do.**
